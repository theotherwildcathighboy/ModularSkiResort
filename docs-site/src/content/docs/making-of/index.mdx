This mod is one person with a game and a lot of opinions (the owner, who decides what the resort should be and
tests everything in the game) working with Claude as lead architect (design, correctness, the written record).
Everything below happened in about eleven weeks. The rule that made it possible: **every
claim about how the engine behaves comes from the decompiled game or a field test, and the written record gets
corrected when the field disagrees.** It has been corrected, twice.

## 1. First Chairlift

The plan on day one was simple: copy how the Modular Golf Course Pack works, but add real ski-resort rules.
The game has no ski lifts, so a lift had to be a *network*, drawn like a road. The first line that drew in the game
came on day two. Then came the slow part: cables that would not connect to stations, towers that floated or
trenched on slopes, and a doctrine we still follow, **anchor as little as possible and let the game's
power-line machinery do the rest**. Dynamic tower heights, foundations and a sagging cable arrived on days four and five,
and by day five chairs rode the cable and power flowed from the grid up the line.

## 2. Hybrid Item

Paradox Mods can host a code mod and an asset pack as separate items, and many projects do. We chose one
**hybrid item** instead: the code and the assets in a single upload. The reason is a save-game detail: the saved
identity of every prefab includes a hash of the item that owns it. Split the pack into two items and every
asset's identity changes, along with every player's saves, the moment anything moves between them. One item,
for the life of the mod, means the identity never moves. That decision is cheap to say and expensive to undo,
so it was made before the first public upload.

## 3. Layered Snow

Our first idea for ski runs was to hijack a terrain-paint slot. It worked, and we rejected it: many maps have no
free slot to take. We took four routes in parallel (writing the game's own snow data directly, decal surfaces,
reading how the terrain splat is encoded, and a mesh that conforms to the ground) and found all four viable. The
answer was not to pick one, but to **layer them**: the snow simulation itself carries the white, decals sell
the groomed corduroy, and the run's rules read the same snow the player sees. That is why a run melts when it
should, why snow guns matter, and why the mod can say *open* and mean it.

## 4. Tile Sheet

Our own review called the stone "blurry". We assumed compression and measured: the real problem was **texel
density**. Our walls had 10 to 46 texels per metre; the game's own buildings have 340 to 520. The Editor binds a
texture to a model only by file-name keyword, and only sixteen keywords exist, so per-material textures are
impossible, and vanilla does not do it either. It paints one big sheet and reuses the same pixels dozens of
times. We copied that: **one shared 4096-pixel tile sheet** of repeating materials for all eight buildings'
nearest detail level, each building keeping its own cheaper maps for distance. Walls went from 10 to 46 to
300 to 400 texels per metre. The price is triangles, because repeating a material means cutting faces into
tiles, and every building now lives under a hard triangle cap.

## 5. Vanilla High-Rise

The tile sheet worked on lodges and then ran into the tram station, a very large building where cutting every
wall into tiles would blow the triangle budget. So we studied how vanilla draws its biggest buildings. The
surprise: they are not sharper than ours were. Big vanilla buildings run at 93 to 304 texels per metre, not 340
to 520, and they reach it by **cutting faces along storeys and bays** and **stacking identical texture islands on
top of each other**, reusing them tens to hundreds of times. Our tram atlas was three-quarters empty. The fix
was to steer the cuts along edges that already exist and stack repeated parts, not to blanket every wall.

## 6. Wheel Rounds

Lifts are mostly wheels. The haul rope runs around a bullwheel at each station and over sheaves on every tower,
and nothing looks wrong faster than a chair that leaves a wheel at an odd angle or a rope that is not tangent to
it. We rebuilt the turn-around more than once: first so chairs follow a path through the wheel's centre, then so the
rope leaves the wheel tangent to the sheave, then so *one* shared builder makes the wheel trains on every station
and tower, with the rope tangent to the millimetre. The tram's drive wheel was a separate lesson in
scale: 10.4 m in our first model, 3.6 m once it matched the real thing.

## 7. Hub Harness

The Ski Resort panel is a web page running inside the game. Testing a button's wording by launching a city and
waiting for it to load does not scale to the hundreds of strings and states a panel like this has. So we built a
**harness**: the same panel bundle, mounted in an ordinary browser, with stand-ins for the game's modules and a
dropdown of scenarios (an empty city, a half-built resort, eight years of history). Wording rounds, layout fixes
and the History view were built and reviewed there first. The harness is not the game and says so; the
game still has the last word.

## 8. Night Shift

In one October night, six sessions worked in parallel from the owner's 24-item field report: windows that
showed the wrong interior (the game picks interiors by keyword, and ours picked a commercial building's),
recolour slots, a tower test that turned out to need our own planner, a ski school that showed "No ski school" when it existed. Each session
had one lane, one report and a rule against touching the others' files. The lesson was not speed. It was that a
long list of small complaints becomes tractable when each one gets a written diagnosis before any fix, and a
check that fails if it comes back.
